Sales Territory Balancer — a Power BI custom visual by TCViz · Last updated: 29 September 2026

Version 1.0.0.0 · See what's new

Give it your points of sale — where they are and how much work each one takes — and it returns compact territories of equal workload, travel time included, and tells you how many salespeople you need. Two levels: first commercial areas that make geographic sense (your own — province, region — or drawn by the visual), then, inside each area, one territory per salesperson. Everything is computed inside Power BI: no external service, no network requests, and the locations of your customers never leave your report. The parameters live in a bar inside the visual, so the people reading the report can try "what if a salesperson had 120 hours" without edit rights. Everything below is documented against version 1.0.0.0 of the visual.

Quick start

  1. Drop the visual on the canvas.
  2. Drag your point-of-sale identifier into Customer ID, and its coordinates into Latitude and Longitude. Customer ID is required: without it, Power BI would aggregate every latitude into one point.
  3. Give each point a workload — Visits per month plus Minutes per visit (the visual adds travel time per visit), or Hours per month if you already have the workload in hours.
  4. Optionally drag your commercial area — province, sales region, whatever you manage by — into Area. Or type a number in Areas in the bar and the visual draws geographic areas for you. Leave both empty for a single plan.
  5. In the bar above the map, set Hours / salesperson / month — field hours available to one salesperson — and the tolerance. The territories appear, and the panel says how many salespeople each area needs.

The control bar

The parameters sit inside the visual, above the map, and work in reading view. In edit mode the values you set are saved with the report so it opens that way for everyone; in reading view a reader can change them freely and nothing is written back.

ControlWhat it does
Hours / salesperson / monthField hours available per month to one salesperson, visits and travel included (e.g. 20 days × 7 h = 140) — the size of a territory. The same for everyone. The number of salespeople is derived from it: the area's workload, travel included, divided by these hours.
± %Tolerance. 10 means every territory should fall between 90% and 110% of the hours. The panel flags those that do not.
km/hAverage travel speed, to turn distance into time, for every point that has no Speed of its own. 45 is a reasonable mix of city and road; a single figure is a rough model — bind Speed per point when you can.
Road ×Road factor over the straight line. 1.3 means "30% longer than the straight line", a common average.
Working days / month (format pane)20 by default. A working day is the hours per salesperson divided by this. The visits that fit in a day share one trip out to the territory and back — see How travel is counted.
How travel is counted. The visual does not compute road routes; it uses the continuous route approximation from logistics (Beardwood–Halton–Hammersley, 1959; Daganzo, 1984), the standard way to plan without routing. Each visit pays Road × (hop to the next client + 2 × distance to the territory centre ÷ visits per day) ÷ speed. The hop is the mean distance to the client's three nearest neighbours; visits per day follow from the month, so a dense urban territory shares the daily trip among eight visits and a rural one among three. Distances are straight lines times the road factor, so the model is for sizing and designing territories, not for planning the day's route.
AreasNumber of commercial areas to draw automatically when no Area field is bound: compact geographic cells weighted by workload, with no size constraint. An area only joins states or provinces that share a land border — it never reaches across a gulf or a sea (Baja California Sur is never grouped with Sinaloa). Islands get an area of their own, on top of the number you type: every land group apart from the main one in your data — Balearic Islands, each Canary province, the Azores, Sicily, Crete, Hawaii — is its own area, named after it, because a salesperson does not drive from Palma to Valencia. Islands that belong to a mainland region (Euboea, Cozumel) stay with it. A salesperson's territory never crosses the sea: inside an area, each island is split on its own, so Las Palmas gives separate territories for Gran Canaria, Lanzarote and Fuerteventura, and a small island shows a partial one ("0.3 salespeople") — someone covers it part-time. Without a postal code, clients within 2 km of each other are kept together as a town, so an area boundary does not split a village. 0 = no areas, one plan for everything.
Outlier kmA point of sale whose third-nearest client is farther than this is an outlier: it is drawn hollow and left out, for you to decide. 0 = no outliers. Every other client always belongs to a territory, so that its hours count in the salespeople needed.
RecalculateRuns the balancing again with the current values. Changing any value and pressing Enter does the same.

The panel

Under the map: how many territories were created, how many points, whether all of them are inside the tolerance band (and if not, how many are outside), what share of total hours is travel, and how many areas. With areas, one chip per area: its name, how many territories (= salespeople) it needs, its hours, and how many of its territories are outside the band. Without areas, one chip per territory with its points, hours and deviation — the ones outside the band come first, marked with ⚠. Hover any point for its customer ID, workload, territory and area.

Export CSV downloads the assignment: customer ID, area, territory number and monthly workload. The file is built inside the visual and handed to Power BI's own download dialog; nothing is uploaded anywhere. See the FAQ if the button says the export was not allowed.

Export map PDF (Pro) saves the map as you see it — outlines, points, area badges and legend — at twice the screen resolution, on a one-page PDF. Power BI does not let a visual download image files such as PNG; a PDF opens anywhere and pastes into a slide. It needs the same tenant setting as the CSV.

How it works, in short

Areas first. If you bind an Area field, those are the areas. Otherwise the visual draws the number you asked for: compact geographic cells around the workload, with no size constraint, so they are convex and never interleave. Then, inside each area, the territories:

Each territory carries a bias. Every point joins the territory that minimises distance² plus bias. If a territory ends up with too much work its bias rises and it attracts less; if it has too little, the bias falls. Iterated, the workloads converge to the capacity and the territories come out compact and convex — no islands, no fingers. Travel time depends on the assignment and the assignment on travel time, so the two are recomputed together until they settle. The number of territories is adjusted between passes until the total fits. That leaves the great majority of territories inside the band; a final repair pass then treats the band as a hard rule — it moves and swaps single points of sale along the borders, and splits or dissolves a territory when nothing else works — until every territory is inside it. Last, a clean-up removes isolated clients: a point whose neighbours all belong to another territory is moved into it, or swapped with a border client of similar workload, whenever both territories stay inside the band. The same data and parameters always give the same result.

Frequently asked questions

A territory is flagged ⚠ outside the tolerance

Some are, and always on the short side: on a 40,000-point test of Spain, 45 of 720 territories end below the band and none above; on 30,000 points across the contiguous United States, 118 of 756 below, none above. The visual never moves a client out of a territory to make the hours fit — that is what leaves clients stranded in a colleague's town. A territory over the cap is split into two compact halves instead, and a short territory is dissolved into its neighbours whenever all of its clients fit under their cap; the ones that remain are those whose neighbours have no room. The rule is asymmetric on purpose — a salesperson with spare hours can be given more later; one at 130% cannot be asked — so a territory that cannot shed a border client is split even if the new one ends short. The panel tells you which side and why. Below: not enough clients within reach — a remote area whose whole workload is less than one salesperson's hours gives one short territory, and that is the honest answer. Above: a single point of sale carries more of the territory than the tolerance allows and cannot be placed anywhere without breaking the band on the other side. Widening the tolerance a few points brings most of them in; the trade-off is that territories get less equal.

Why areas first, instead of asking for big territories?

Because balancing hours and keeping geography sensible pull in opposite directions, and at the scale of a region the hours must lose. A region is a management unit — "Aragón, +12%" is useful; "Aragón minus two towns that go with Catalonia" is not. So areas are drawn (or taken from your field) with no size constraint, which is what makes them compact and whole, and the hours are balanced where it matters: between the salespeople inside each area.

Export CSV says the export was not allowed or not completed

Downloads from custom visuals are governed by a Power BI tenant setting that is off by default and applies to Power BI Desktop as well as the service: Allow downloads from custom visuals, under Tenant settings → Power BI visuals in the Fabric admin portal. Ask your administrator to enable it (it is separate from the general export settings), make sure you are signed in to Power BI, and try again. Before the first download Power BI asks you to confirm you trust the visual. Nothing about your data leaves the visual in any of this: the file is generated locally and handed to Power BI's own save dialog.

Why is there no street map behind the points?

A street map means fetching tiles from a third-party server, and every tile request would tell that server which area you are looking at. For a visual that handles customer addresses, that information should stay in your report. So the visual makes no network requests at all. What it does draw is embedded outlines — country borders and first-level regions (states, provinces, departments) for every country — from Natural Earth (public domain), packaged inside the visual and simplified to a few kilometres. Only the outlines around your data are drawn. Enough to know where you are, not a street map.

Are the distances real road distances?

No — straight line multiplied by the road factor. Real routing needs an online service, which would mean network access. A factor of 1.3 is a common average for mixed road networks; raise it in mountainous or coastal areas, lower it on grids.

Can the visual write the territories into my model?

No. Power BI custom visuals can read the data they are given but cannot modify the dataset. The visual is a design and simulation tool: tune, look, decide, then carry the assignment across with the CSV export.

How many points can it handle?

All of them. Power BI hands the rows over in blocks of 30,000; the visual asks for the next block until it has every row ("Loading points… 30,000 so far"), then balances the full set ("Balancing 41,260 points…"). The only ceiling is the memory Power BI gives a visual. Balancing 40,000 points takes about 20 seconds in a browser; a typical few thousand takes a second or two.

Everything collapsed into a single point

Customer ID is not bound, so Power BI aggregated every latitude into one value. Bind an identifier with one row per point of sale.

The number of territories seems wrong

It is total workload divided by capacity, travel included. If it looks too high, check the units: a Value in minutes instead of hours makes every point sixty times heavier.
