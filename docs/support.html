Sales Territory Balancer — a Power BI custom visual by TCViz · Last updated: 7 October 2026

Version 1.0.0.0 Methodology: how every number is computed What's new

Give it your points of sale — where they are and how much work each one takes — and it tells you how many salespeople you need and proposes a territory for each one. It works on two levels: first commercial areas (your own — province, region, delegation — or drawn by the visual), then, inside each area, territories of at most one salesperson's monthly hours, travel included. Everything is computed inside Power BI: no external service, no network requests, and the locations of your customers never leave your report. The parameters live in a bar inside the visual, so readers can try "what if a salesperson had 120 hours" in reading view.

This page is the user guide. The algorithms, formulas, assumptions and references are in the Methodology.

Quick start

  1. Drop the visual on the canvas.
  2. Drag your point-of-sale identifier into Customer ID, and its coordinates into Latitude and Longitude. Use one row per point of sale: without Customer ID, Power BI aggregates all the coordinates into a single point.
  3. Give each point a workload: Visits per month and Minutes per visit. The visual adds the travel time.
  4. Define the areas: drag your commercial area into Area Pro, or type a number in Areas in the bar and the visual draws them. Until there are areas the map shows your points and asks for them.
  5. Set Hours / salesperson / month in the bar. Each area's badge and the legend show the salespeople it needs; hover any point for its territory.

The control bar

The bar sits inside the visual, above the map, and works in reading view. In edit mode its values are saved with the report; in reading view a reader can change them and nothing is written back.

ControlWhat it does
Hours / salesperson / monthField hours available per month to one salesperson, visits and travel included (e.g. 20 days × 7 h = 140). It is the size of a territory — a hard cap — and the salespeople needed are the workload divided by it.
km/hAverage travel speed for every point without a Speed of its own. 45 is a reasonable mix of town and road.
Road ×Road distance over straight-line distance. Set it for your country: published averages are about 1.2 for the United States, 1.3 for India, 1.5 for Mexico and 1.6 for Spain (see the Methodology). Higher means more travel and more salespeople.
AreasNumber of commercial areas to draw automatically when no Area field is bound. They follow geography and do not balance hours: an area that needs 12 salespeople next to one that needs 2 is a correct answer. Areas only join regions that share a land border, so they never reach across a gulf or the sea; islands and archipelagos (Balearic Islands, each Canary province, Azores, Sicily, Crete, Hawaii…) get an area of their own, named after them, on top of the number you type. 0 = nothing is computed.
RecalculateRuns the calculation again. Changing a value and pressing Enter does the same.
Export CSV ProDownloads customer_id, area, territory, load_hours_month for every client, plus postal_code when bound.
Export map PDF ProSaves the map as you see it — outlines, points, area badges and legend — at twice the screen resolution, on a one-page PDF. PDF because Power BI does not let a visual download image files such as PNG; it pastes into a slide and prints well.

Reading the result

  • Colours are areas. Points and badges shrink as you zoom out.
  • Badge on each area: salespeople it needs, to one decimal (6.4, not "6 or 7": rounding is your decision). A small area or island can show a fraction such as 0.3 — someone covers it part-time.
  • Legend: salespeople needed per area and in total. Click a row or a badge to focus that area; other visuals on the page are filtered to it.
  • Tooltip on any point: customer, monthly workload split into visit and travel, territory number, area and postal code.
  • Territories are not drawn in their own colour — there can be hundreds. Each client's territory number is in its tooltip and in the CSV export. A territory never exceeds the hours in the bar, never crosses an area boundary and never crosses the sea.

How it works, in short

Each client's workload is its visit time plus travel. Travel is estimated, not routed: each visit pays the hop to the next client (the mean distance to its three nearest clients) and its share of one trip a day from the territory's base and back, shared among the visits that fit in a day — the continuous approximation of routing used in logistics planning. Distances are straight lines times the road factor. Areas come from your field or from weighted k-means that respects land borders. Inside each area and each island, territories are built from the towns outward and balanced with a power diagram, with one salesperson's hours as a hard cap. Salespeople needed = workload ÷ hours per salesperson. The same data and parameters always give the same result. The full explanation, with formulas and references, is in the Methodology.

Frequently asked questions

Why does nothing happen when I only add coordinates?

Territories are built inside areas, so the visual waits until there are areas: bind an Area field or type a number in Areas. Until then it shows your points in grey and says so.

Some clients are in the "wrong" area when I use my Area field

With the Area field, each client goes exactly where your data puts it — the visual never overrides it, because a company may assign a town across a provincial border on purpose. If a client labelled "Madrid" sits in Guadalajara, check the value in your data. For areas drawn purely by location, leave Area empty and type a number in Areas.

Why areas first, instead of asking for big territories?

Because balancing hours and keeping geography sensible pull in opposite directions, and at the scale of a region the hours must lose. A region is a management unit — "Aragón needs 12.4" is useful; "Aragón minus two towns that go with Catalonia" is not. So areas follow geography with no size constraint, and the hours are balanced where it matters: between the salespeople inside each area.

Are the distances real road distances?

No — straight line multiplied by the road factor, which you set for your country. Real routing needs an online service, which would mean sending your customers' locations over the network. The model is for sizing and designing territories, not for planning the day's route; see the assumptions and limitations.

Export says it was not allowed or not completed

Downloads from custom visuals are governed by a Power BI tenant setting that is off by default and applies to Power BI Desktop as well as the service: Allow downloads from custom visuals, under Tenant settings → Power BI visuals in the Fabric admin portal. Ask your administrator to enable it, make sure you are signed in to Power BI, and try again. Before the first download Power BI asks you to confirm you trust the visual. The file is generated inside the visual and handed to Power BI's own save dialog; nothing is uploaded anywhere.

Why is there no street map behind the points?

A street map means fetching tiles from a third-party server, and every tile request would tell that server which area you are looking at. So the visual makes no network requests at all and draws embedded outlines instead: country borders and first-level regions (states, provinces, departments) for every country, from Natural Earth, simplified to a few kilometres. Only the outlines around your data are drawn.

Can the visual write the territories into my model?

No. Power BI custom visuals can read the data they are given but cannot modify the dataset. The visual is a design and simulation tool: tune, look, decide, then carry the assignment across with the CSV export.

How many points can it handle?

All of them. Power BI hands the rows over in blocks of 30,000; the visual asks for the next block until it has every row ("Loading points… 30,000 so far"), then computes on the full set ("Balancing 41,260 points…"). The only ceiling is the memory Power BI gives a visual. On the sample data, 20,000 clients take about 5 seconds and 41,000 about 10; a few thousand take a second or two.

Everything collapsed into a single point

Customer ID is not bound, so Power BI aggregated every coordinate into one value. Bind an identifier with one row per point of sale.

The number of salespeople seems wrong

It is workload ÷ hours per salesperson, travel included. Check the units: Minutes per visit must be minutes, and Hours / salesperson / month must be monthly hours. Then check the road factor and speed: they decide the travel share, which you can see per client in the tooltip.
