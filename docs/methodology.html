Methodology

Sales Territory Balancer — a Power BI custom visual by TCViz · How every number is computed

Version 1.0.0.0Last updated: 7 October 2026 User guide

This page is written for people who want to know exactly what the visual computes before they trust it: analysts, operations researchers, sales-operations leads. It describes the algorithms as they run in version 1.0.0.0, the formulas and constants they use, the assumptions behind them and what they do not do. Where a choice comes from the literature, the reference is given; where it is our own engineering, it says so. Nothing here is aspirational: if the code does not do it, this page does not claim it.

In one paragraph. Each client's monthly workload is its visit time plus a travel estimate from the continuous approximation of routing (Beardwood–Halton–Hammersley; Daganzo). The workload is grouped into commercial areas — yours, or drawn automatically with weighted k-means++ constrained by land borders — and, inside each area and each landmass, into territories that each fit one salesperson's monthly hours. The number of salespeople is the workload divided by those hours: the classic workload approach to sales-force sizing (Talley, 1961). It is a sizing and design tool. It does not plan routes.

1. Scope: what it answers, what it does not

Territory design is usually split into two questions (Zoltners & Sinha, 2005): how many salespeople are needed (sizing), and which clients each one covers (alignment). The visual answers both, in that order, at planning level:

  • Sizing. Salespeople needed per area and in total, as fractional full-time equivalents (FTE) — 6.4, not "6 or 7" — so the decision of how to round stays with you.
  • Alignment. A proposed territory for every client: compact, never above one salesperson's monthly hours, never across an area boundary or the sea.

It does not plan the day's route, schedule visits, model time windows or visit frequencies that vary by week, or read a road network. Distances are straight lines scaled by a road factor. That is the standard simplification for planning-level models (§4), and it is the reason the visual can run inside Power BI with no external service.

2. The pipeline

1 Points & workload→ 2 Outliers (optional)→ 3 Region & landmass of each point→ 4 Commercial areas→ 5 Territories per area × landmass→ 6 Salespeople needed

Nothing is computed until areas exist: either a field in Area or a number in the bar's Areas box. With only coordinates, the map shows the points and asks for areas. Each step is described below.

Power BI hands rows over in blocks of 30,000; the visual requests the next block until it has every row (fetchMoreData), then computes on the full set.

3.2 Visit workload

For client i with vi visits per month of mi minutes:

Visit time per month (minutes) Vi = vi · mi

Defaults when fields are missing: no visits → 1 visit; no minutes → 60 / vi minutes. With neither, every client counts as one hour of visit time per month, and the result is a geographic split of the clients rather than a workload plan.

3.3 Outliers (optional)

If you set Outlier km in the format pane, a client whose third nearest client is farther than that straight-line distance is drawn hollow, labelled outlier in the export and left out of every area and territory — its hours do not count. Using the third neighbour means an isolated pair or trio is still flagged, while a village of four is not. The default is 0 (off).

4. Travel: continuous approximation

Travel depends on the territory and the territory depends on travel, so an exact answer would need a route for every candidate territory. Planning models avoid that with the continuous approximation of routing (Daganzo, 1984a; Ansari et al., 2018): the length of a route that visits many points in a region is predicted from the number of points and the area they cover, without solving the route. The visual uses its two classic terms.

4.1 Coordinates and road distance

Coordinates are projected to kilometres with an equirectangular projection centred on the mean latitude of the points being computed (an error of tenths of a percent at the scale of a country, small next to the road factor). A straight-line distance d becomes travel time with

Minutes per straight-line km τi = r / si × 60

where r is the road (circuity) factor from the bar and si the speed (the client's own Speed if given, else the bar value).

4.2 The hop to the next client

Beardwood, Halton & Hammersley (1959) proved that the shortest tour through n random points in an area A has length ≈ β √(n A); per point that is β / √ρ with density ρ = n/A. The best estimate of the planar constant is β ≈ 0.7124 (Johnson, McGeoch & Rothberg, 1996; independently 0.7120, Percus & Martin, 1996). Rather than estimating densities, the visual measures the local spacing directly:

Hop per visit (minutes) hi = τi · ⅓ ∑j ∈ NN3(i) ‖pi − pj‖

the mean straight-line distance to the three nearest clients. For points scattered at random (a Poisson process of density ρ) the expected distances to the 1st, 2nd and 3rd neighbours are 0.5, 0.75 and 0.9375 / √ρ, whose mean is 0.729 / √ρ — the same order as the BHH constant, but measured on your data instead of assumed. Clustered clients get short hops, scattered ones long hops.

4.3 The daily trip, shared by the visits of the day

Daganzo (1984a) splits the distance of a tour into a line-haul part — getting from the depot to the zone and back, once per tour — and the local part of §4.2. Here the depot is the territory's base and a tour is a working day. The number of visits that fit in a day is not a parameter: it follows from the month.

Working day and visits per day D = C / W   ·   qi = max(1, D / ui)

C is the salesperson's monthly hours (bar, in minutes), W the working days per month (format pane, default 20, so 140 h → 7 h a day) and ui the mean visit time (m + h) over client i and its 30 nearest clients, weighted by visits. A dense urban zone with short visits fits about eight a day; a rural one with long visits, about three. The estimate leaves the day's own trip out of ui, so it slightly overestimates q; it is never below one.

4.4 Monthly workload of a client

Monthly workload of client i in a territory with base c (minutes) Li(c) = vi · ( mi + hi + d(i, c) · 2 τi / qi )

Each visit pays its hop and its share of one return trip from the base. A territory's workload is ∑ Li; its hours are that over 60.

Why not the simpler "trip from the centre for every visit"? Earlier builds charged each visit the trip from the territory centre, as if the salesperson drove back to base between clients: ten clients along one road paid ten trips instead of one. On the four sample datasets that asked for 6–15% more salespeople than the route-based model (Spain 679 → 636, United States 637 → 555, Mexico 412 → 361, India 676 → 578, at 140 h, 45 km/h, road × 1.3).

4.5 The road factor

The ratio between road and straight-line distance varies by country and terrain, so it is a bar parameter (1.0–3.0, default 1.3) for you to set. Published average circuity factors give a starting point:

CountryAverage factorSource
United States1.20Ballou, Rahardja & Sakai (2002)
United States (trips to hospital)1.42Boscoe, Henry & Zdeb (2012)
India1.31Ballou et al. (2002)

Ballou's figures are inter-city averages with a wide spread (standard deviation up to 0.8), quoted here as they are commonly reproduced; check the original table before relying on a single value. Local factors can differ; if you can compute your own ratio from a sample of real trips, use it. Raising the factor raises travel time, and with it the salespeople needed — the legend updates as you type.

5. Sales-force sizing

The visual applies the workload approach (Talley, 1961; Zoltners & Sinha, 2005): salespeople needed = total workload ÷ the workload one salesperson can carry.

Salespeople needed in area a Na = ∑i ∈ a Li ⁄ C

Shown to one decimal on the map (one badge per area) and in the legend, with the total. Every client counts: a remnant that fits in no full territory becomes a partial territory (§7.4), so a small island shows, for instance, 0.3 salespeople. Only clients you flag as outliers (§3.3) are excluded. Because Li depends on the territory's base, Na is computed after the territories (§7), not before.

6. Commercial areas

Areas are the level at which you decide headcount: a province, a region, a delegation. They follow geography and do not balance hours — an area that needs 12 salespeople next to one that needs 2 is a correct answer, not a defect.

6.1 Your own areas (Area field)

Each client goes to its own Area value; the postal code plays no part. The visual never second-guesses the Area column: a client labelled "Madrid" that sits in Guadalajara stays in Madrid, because a company may assign a town across a provincial border on purpose.

6.2 Units: postal codes or towns

Automatic areas are built from units, never from loose clients, so that an area boundary does not cut a village in half. The postal code does not make the areas better — on the sample data the same areas come out with and without it (99–100% of the workload) — but it makes them usable: each area is a set of whole postal codes, the unit in which many companies assign territories, and the CSV export carries each client's code.

  • With a postal code, a unit is a postal code. Two safeguards apply. A code whose clients span more than 25 km (typically a capital's code given to a whole province) is split into its towns. And the visual checks that the column is geographic at all: it measures the share of clients whose nearest client has the same code (0.86–0.91 on real-style data); below 0.5 the column is ignored, units are built as without a postal code, and the map says so.
  • Without a postal code, a unit is a town: clients linked by Delaunay edges of at most 2 km, plus clients at identical coordinates. A town wider than 25 km (a conurbation) is cut into 5 km cells.

6.3 Grouping: weighted k-means++

Units are grouped into the requested number of areas K by k-means (Lloyd, 1982) weighted by visit time, seeded with k-means++ (Arthur & Vassilvitskii, 2007), minimising

Objective ∑u wu · ‖xu − ca(u)‖²

with 24 restarts (at most 50 Lloyd iterations each) and the lowest objective kept. With a single start, binding a postal code or not left only 68–73% of the workload in the same area when five areas were requested, because several groupings are almost equally good; with 24 restarts, 99–100%.

6.4 Land borders and islands

Straight-line k-means happily groups the two shores of a gulf. Two rules prevent it, both from the embedded regional outlines (§8):

  • Land groups. Regions connected by land borders form a land group (the Iberian peninsula with France and the rest of Europe is one; the Balearic Islands, each Canary province, the Azores, Sicily, Crete or Hawaii are others). The land group with most workload is grouped into the K areas; every other land group becomes an area of its own, named after its region, on top of K. A salesperson does not drive from Palma to Valencia.
  • Contiguity. The neighbourhood graph between units is the Delaunay triangulation of the clients (Delaunay, 1934), keeping only edges between regions that share a land border (or the same region). An area split into pieces moves each smaller piece wholly to the area it shares most edges with (Hess & Samuels, 1971, on contiguity as a design criterion). A piece with no edge at all (a stretch of coast hundreds of kilometres from the next client) goes to the nearest unit of a region with a land border, else of the same land group.

6.5 Stragglers: nearest-neighbour vote

Boundaries from k-means run between centres and can leave a handful of clients on the wrong side of a town. A final pass lets every small unit (at most 30 clients) vote: it looks at the 10 nearest clients outside it (searching up to ~120 km), weights each by 1 / (d + 0.5 km), and if more than 60% of the weight belongs to another area with a land border, it moves there. Those neighbour links (≤ 10 km) also count as contiguity, so the repair of §6.4 does not move the unit back. Up to five passes. Ordinary boundaries sit near 50% and are left alone.

7. Territories

Inside every area, and separately for every landmass in it (§8.3), the clients are split into territories. Each territory is one salesperson's month: its workload never exceeds C — a hard cap, not a tolerance band — and the number of territories is not asked for.

7.1 Towns first

Dense clusters of clients are found by linking each client to those of its 10 nearest neighbours closer than twice the median distance to the 5th neighbour. A cluster with at least half a salesperson's workload is a town and seeds territories. A town larger than one salesperson is split with the power diagram of §7.3 restricted to its own clients; any part that still exceeds the cap is bisected along its principal axis, at the point where the cumulative workload reaches the target. A town is therefore served by whole territories rather than shared with the countryside around it.

7.2 The countryside

Remaining clients are assigned, nearest first, to the closest town territory that still has room (among the 8 nearest). What is left after that is split by the power diagram of §7.3.

7.3 Power diagram with dual ascent

Balanced partitions of points correspond to power diagrams — Voronoi diagrams with an additive weight per cell (Aurenhammer, 1987; Brieden, Gritzmann & Klemm, 2017). Each territory t gets a centre ct and a weight wt; each client goes to

Assignment t(i) = arg mint ‖pi − ct‖² + wt

A territory above its target raises its weight and loses its farthest clients; one below lowers it (dual ascent: wt += 0.1 · st · clamp(load/target − 1, ±0.5), with st the territory's mean squared radius, and |wt| ≤ 20 st). Cells of a power diagram are convex, so the territories come out compact. The loop alternates with Lloyd re-centring (damped by half), up to 150 weight iterations per step, 12 steps per round and 6 rounds. Between rounds, empty centres are moved into saturated territories, territories above the cap are split along their principal axis, and short ones that fit in their neighbours are dropped. The initial centres come from growth: from the densest unassigned client outward, taking the nearest clients until one salesperson's workload is reached (within 100 km). A local search then moves clients whose 10 nearest neighbours are mostly in another territory, when the cap allows (single moves, swaps and two-step chains).

7.4 Post-processing

  1. Reclaim. A client moves to a closer territory with room; if none has room, a chain of up to six evictions toward territories with room is tried, accepted only if it shortens the total distance.
  2. Cap safety. Any territory above the cap gives its farthest clients to the nearest territory with room.
  3. Merge. A territory below the cap is dissolved if all its clients fit, nearest first, into neighbouring territories.
  4. Remnants. Small remnants (up to six clients) are retried client by client, with chains, all or nothing. What still fits nowhere stays as a partial territory: its hours count, and the badge shows the fraction of a salesperson it needs.

The base of a territory — the point the daily trip of §4.3 starts from — is the visit-weighted centroid of its seed group and is not moved afterwards. Territories never cross an area boundary or the sea, and are not drawn with their own colour: points are coloured by area, and each client's territory number is in the tooltip and the CSV export.

8. Geography: outlines, regions, islands

8.1 Embedded outlines

The visual makes no network request, so it carries its own map: country borders from Natural Earth at 1:110m and first-level regions (states, provinces, departments) for every country except Antarctica from Natural Earth 1:10m, simplified with Douglas–Peucker to about 2 km for the United States, Spain, India and Mexico and about 5 km elsewhere. Only the rings around your data are drawn. Natural Earth draws borders de facto; in disputed regions they may differ from a country's official map.

8.2 Region of each point

Each client is placed in the region whose ring contains it (even–odd ray casting, with a bounding-box and 1° grid prefilter). Simplification can leave a coastal or border client outside every ring; it then takes the region of its nearest client that has one.

8.3 Land borders and landmasses

Two regions are neighbours if their unsimplified 1:10m boundaries share at least two vertices (simplified rings no longer meet exactly). From that graph come the land groups of §6.4. A finer notion, the landmass, joins rings that share a boundary: Gran Canaria, Lanzarote and Fuerteventura are one region (Las Palmas) but three landmasses, while Michigan's Upper Peninsula is joined to the mainland through Wisconsin. Territories are computed per landmass (§7).

10. Determinism and performance

Determinism. Clients are sorted by Customer ID before anything is computed, and every random choice uses a seeded generator (mulberry32) whose seed depends only on the area and landmass. The same data and parameters always give the same areas and territories, whatever order Power BI delivers the rows in. Without a Customer ID, ties fall back to row order.

Performance (measured in Node.js on the sample datasets of §12):

StepClientsTime
Region of every point20,000–40,000≈ 30 ms
Automatic areas (5–7)20,000–41,0000.15–0.45 s
Territories, Mexico, 5 automatic areas20,0005.1 s
Territories, Spain with islands, 7 automatic areas (10 with islands)41,26010.5 s

A few thousand clients take a second or two. The neighbour searches use spatial grids, so the cost grows roughly as n × (neighbours examined), not n²; the dominant term is the power diagram, bounded by its fixed iteration limits.

11. Assumptions and limitations

  • Straight lines × road factor. No road network: mountains, rivers without bridges or ferries are not seen, except that areas and territories never cross the sea or join regions without a land border. Set the road factor for your country (§4.5).
  • Planning, not routing. Travel is an expected value from the continuous approximation; any single day's route can be longer or shorter.
  • One base per territory, at the centre of its seed group; salespeople's home addresses are not used.
  • Visits are independent and steady: no time windows, no weekly patterns, no priorities between clients.
  • One speed per client (Pro) or one for everyone.
  • Hard cap. Territories can be short, never long; the remainder of an area forms a partial territory.
  • Areas follow geography, not workload balance.
  • Simplified outlines (2–5 km) decide the region of points within a few kilometres of a border; it matters only for the land-border rules.
  • Memory. Power BI limits the memory a visual may use; very large models may not deliver every row.

12. Validation

The algorithms were checked on four synthetic datasets of points of sale distributed by population (Spain 40,000 clients, United States 30,000, Mexico 20,000, India 30,000), plus Spain with clients on the Balearic and Canary Islands, and with deliberately dirty data (postal codes that span whole provinces, province labels taken from the nearest city).
